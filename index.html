Mission

A real satellite architecture that a university team can afford to build.

CubeSats are made from standard 10 cm units. Their small size and low cost let universities test space technology that used to be out of reach.

QuanSat is a prototype that reproduces the core subsystems of a flight CubeSat: power, on-board computing, communication, payload, attitude control, thermal management and structure, plus a ground station. It is a platform for learning and research in satellite system design, with room to try intelligent power management and alternative communication methods.

The goal is a practical framework for the design problems small satellites raise, and a low-cost route into satellite research for teams with limited resources.

On-board edge AI

The satellite decides what is worth sending down.

A lightweight model sits between the camera and the radio and judges every image in orbit. Cloudy and empty frames stay on board. Only a short result record, and the image itself when the model flags it, goes into the downlink queue.

That matters because a Cairo pass lasts only a few hundred seconds. The module draws power through the power subsystem and talks over the same bus as everything else, so it needs no redesign of the satellite.

Data flow for each capture

  1. ScheduleThe OBC schedules a capture and powers the payload.
  2. Capture and classifyThe camera takes an image and the model runs on it.
  3. Return a recordThe payload sends back class, confidence, time, position and a quality flag, then powers down.
  4. Store selectivelyThe OBC keeps the record. It keeps the raw image only if it is flagged, or if a periodic random sample is due.
  5. Downlink in orderOn the next pass the radio sends records first, then flagged thumbnails if time remains.
  6. Ground can ask for moreThe ground station can command a raw image downlink for any record.

Use case

Decide whether each image is usable and what it shows, such as cloudy or clear, land or water. A second, lighter task on the OBC flags anomalies in housekeeping telemetry instead of streaming every sample.

Model

A compact convolutional network of the MobileNet class, trained on public satellite image sets such as EuroSAT, quantized to INT8 and run with TensorFlow Lite.

Why the model is frozen before launch

At about 1 kbps, uplinking a 1 MB model takes roughly 2.3 hours of continuous link, around 20 passes. Only thresholds and class priorities are commanded from the ground.

Mission analysis

How often we can talk to the satellite, and how long it sits in shadow.

We simulated the orbit in NASA's General Mission Analysis Tool (GMAT) with one spacecraft, a ground station in Cairo, a contact locator and an eclipse locator.

Two results drive the biggest design choices. Pass length sets the link budget, which is how much data fits in one pass. Eclipse length sets the power budget, which is how large the battery must be.

Pass length sets the data per pass

At 1 kbps, the rate used in our uplink estimate, a 308 to 431 second pass carries roughly 38 to 54 kB. That is why the AI payload filters images before anything joins the downlink queue.

Eclipse length sets the battery size

During the longest shadow of about 35 minutes, the battery carries the whole satellite. The power subsystem is sized for that case.

These figures come from a single one-day run. Eclipse length changes with the seasons, so the analysis will be repeated over a longer period.
